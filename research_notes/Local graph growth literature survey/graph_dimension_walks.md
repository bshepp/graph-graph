# Measuring the dimension of graphs: estimators, finite-size pitfalls, shortcuts, tree-likeness, and walks

Scope: how the literature defines and estimates graph dimension (Hausdorff/volume-growth,
spectral, box-covering), the known failure modes on finite and tree-like graphs, how
small-world shortcuts change dimension and walk behaviour, localization of walks on trees,
connectivity thresholds, and Laplacian spectral signatures — mapped onto the graph-graph
sandbox (d_eff = slope of log|B(v,r)| vs log r; window-stability audit; `grown` has no
dimension; portals destroy dimension at ~20 shortcuts / 5000 nodes; 100x walk gains on
tree-like vs 1.0–1.4x on 2D; smooth d(p) crossover; throat threshold ~2.6 strands;
Lorentzian spikes in the Laplacian spectrum).

Notation used throughout: N = node count; |B(v,r)| = number of nodes within graph distance
r of v; d_H / d_f = Hausdorff / fractal (volume-growth) dimension; d_s = spectral
dimension; d_B = box-covering dimension; d_w = walk dimension; L = Laplacian = D − A.
Mapping labels: **direct** = same object/estimator as the project; **analogous** =
same mechanism in a different model; **speculative** = plausible, not demonstrated.
"[unverified]" marks details I could not confirm from the source itself.

## Key Question 1 — Definitions and estimators of graph dimension; robustness on tree-like graphs; is there an explicit warning that a finite log-log window yields a spurious finite dimension for exponential growth?

### Takeaway
The literature uses three families of estimator — volume growth |B(v,r)| ~ r^d_H
(Hausdorff/"connectivity"/"cluster-growing" dimension), spectral dimension from return
probability P(t) ~ t^(−d_s/2) (equivalently Laplacian DOS ρ(λ) ~ λ^(d_s/2−1)), and
box-covering N_B(l_B) ~ l_B^(−d_B) — and they do **not** agree on small-world/tree-like
graphs: the cluster-growing dimension is formally infinite for exponential growth
(Song–Havlin–Makse; Csányi–Szendrői), while box-covering can still return a finite
exponent. Newman–Watts give the only explicit closed-form statement of the project's
failure mode: on a small-world graph the local effective dimension D(r) = r·A(r)/V(r) is
not a constant but grows linearly, D(r) → 4r/ξ for r ≫ ξ, so any fixed window reports a
finite number that is just the window's position.

### Cited Findings
- **Csányi & Szendrői (2004), "The fractal/small-world dichotomy in real-world networks", arXiv cond-mat/0406070 (published Phys. Rev. E 70, 016122 [journal ref unverified from the page]).** Defines N_v(r) = nodes within r steps of v; fractal dimension d = lim log N_v(r)/log r (N_v ~ r^d) versus growth rate α = lim log N_v(r)/r (N_v ~ e^(αr)); states "if a finite, nonzero limit for d exists, then α=∞; conversely, if α is finite then d=0", i.e. a graph cannot have both; notes that on finite networks scaling holds only over 1 < r < L, that saturation makes exponential scaling detectable only in large networks, and that for networks with mean distance < 10 there are "only two or three useful data points"; the Watts–Strogatz model with small rewiring is "Euclidean (fractal) at small scales and small-world at large scales". — [Source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0406070). **Mapping: direct.** This is the project's exact estimator (|B(v,r)| growth) and the exact dichotomy behind the 2026-09-26 audit: `grown` has finite α, hence d = 0 in the limit — the fixed-window reading was reporting a window artifact.
- **Newman & Watts (1999), "Scaling and percolation in the small-world network model", Phys. Rev. E 60, 7332–7342, arXiv cond-mat/9904419, DOI 10.1103/PhysRevE.60.7332.** Neighbourhood volume obeys the self-consistent relation V(r) = Σ_{r'=0}^{r} a(r')[1 + 2ξ^(−d) V(r−r')]; in d=1, k=1 this gives V(r) = (ξ/2)(e^(4r/ξ) − 1), A(r) = 2e^(4r/ξ); V grows like r for r ≪ ξ and exponentially for r ≫ ξ; the local effective dimension D(r) = r·A(r)/V(r) = (4r/ξ)e^(4r/ξ)/(e^(4r/ξ) − 1) tends to 1 for r ≪ ξ and to 4r/ξ for r ≫ ξ — the authors call this scale dependence "reminiscent of multifractals". — [Source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/9904419). **Mapping: direct.** This is the closest thing in the literature to an explicit "a fixed window gives a spurious finite dimension" warning: beyond ξ, D(r) has no plateau, so a two-point slope reports ≈ 4r/ξ, a number set by where you looked. It is also the formula behind finding (a) ("destroys rather than inflates").
- **Song, Havlin & Makse (2005), "Self-similarity of complex networks", Nature 433, 392–395, arXiv cond-mat/0503078.** Box-covering: N_B(l_B) ~ l_B^(−d_B) (minimum number of boxes of diameter l_B); cluster-growing: ⟨M_c⟩ ~ l^(d_f) for balls grown from random seeds; "comparing [the two] implies d_f = ∞ for small-world networks" because ball growth is exponential; reported d_B: WWW 4.1, actor network 6.3, E. coli and H. sapiens PIN 2.3, cellular networks 3.5; degree-renormalisation exponent d_k with γ = 1 + d_B/d_k. Explanation for why ball growth fails: hubs are near every node, so randomly seeded balls swallow hubs and the average is biased to exponential growth; box-covering tiles the graph so each node is covered once. — [Source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0503078). **Mapping: direct for the d_f = ∞ statement; analogous for the hub mechanism** (the project's `grown` graphs are degree-capped, so the hub-bias explanation does not apply; the exponential growth there is branching, not hubs).
- **Song, Gallos, Havlin & Makse (2007), "How to calculate the fractal dimension of a complex network: the box covering algorithm", J. Stat. Mech. P03006, arXiv cond-mat/0701216.** Maps minimum box covering to graph colouring (greedy colouring gives an approximate minimal cover); exponents found not to depend on the covering choice. A later result cited in the same search: minimum l-box covering is NP-complete for l ≥ 2. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0701216). **Mapping: analogous** — gives the project a second, independent estimator (d_B) that is not a ball-growth slope.
- **Wei, Deng, Mahadevan et al. (2013), "Box-covering algorithm for fractal dimension of weighted networks", Sci. Rep. 3, 3049.** Modifies box covering for weighted networks (box size from edge weights, colouring ordered by node strength); edge weights change the measured fractal dimension. — [Source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6505717). **Mapping: speculative** — only relevant if the project ever wants a dimension that sees the `reinforcement` edge weights.
- **Shanker (2007), "Defining dimension of a complex network", Mod. Phys. Lett. B 21(6), 321–326, DOI 10.1142/S0217984907012773; and "Graph zeta function and dimension of complex network", MPLB 21(11), 639–644.** Defines a volume dimension from the scaling of node "volume" with distance, then generalises via a graph zeta function ζ_G(α) = Σ_{i≠j} d(i,j)^(−α) whose convergence abscissa is the dimension. — [Source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_network_zeta_function). **Mapping: analogous.** The zeta estimator is a global integral of the distance distribution rather than a local slope; **[formulas unverified from the paper itself]**.
- **Daqing (Li), Kosmidis, Bunde & Havlin (2011), "Dimension of spatially embedded networks", Nat. Phys. 7, 481–484.** Spatially embedded networks with broad link-length distribution P(r) ~ r^(−δ) can have dimension higher than the embedding space; argues the airline network and Internet behave as d ≈ 3 and ≈ 4.5 respectively, and that d controls diffusion and percolation. — [Source](https://research.buaa.edu.cn/zh/publications/dimension-of-spatially-embedded-networks/). The follow-up **"Measuring the dimension of partially embedded networks" (arXiv 1308.6075)** gives the operational estimator: P_0(t) = ⟨p_ii(t)⟩ ~ t^(−α), r(t) ~ t^β, hence P_0 ~ r^(−γ) with d_ρ = γ = α/β (d_ρ = d_s = d, β = 1/2 on d-dim lattices); in their 2D model d_ρ ≈ 4.61, 2.17, 1.97 for δ = 2, 3, 4 and **no power law** (exponential decay) for δ = 1 and for the real networks. — [Source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1308.6075). **Mapping: analogous.** The exact piecewise δ→d relation of the Nat. Phys. paper is **[unverified; paywalled]**. Note the embedding-space version needs node coordinates, which the project deliberately lacks; only the chemical-distance / return-probability forms are admissible.
- **Craig, Yücel, Muchnik & Hershberg (2022), "Impact of finite size effect on applicability of generalized fractal and spectral dimensions to biological networks", Chaos, Solitons & Fractals 164, 112707.** Box-covering and sandbox estimators of generalised fractal dimension are strongly size-dependent on real networks (mostly < 10^4 nodes); proposes a memory-biased-random-walk spectral dimension that orders networks more consistently across sizes. — [Source](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/chsofr/v164y2022ics0960077922008864.html). **Mapping: analogous** — direct evidence that N ~ 5000 is in the regime where every estimator is size-biased.
- **Burioni & Cassi (2005), "Random walks on graphs: ideas, techniques and results", J. Phys. A 38, R45–R78, arXiv cond-mat/0507142.** Defines the connectivity (growth) dimension d_g from |V_{o,r}| < c r^k (upper/lower growth exponents; equals the Euclidean d on lattices), the local spectral dimension d̃ from P_ii(t) ~ t^(−d̃/2) and the average d̄ from the site-averaged return probability; d̃ is site-independent when it exists, d̄ can differ on inhomogeneous graphs; **the Bethe lattice "does not satisfy the polynomial growth condition, so its local spectral dimension is infinite" and its return probability decays exponentially**. — [Source](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0507142). **Mapping: direct.** The "polynomial growth condition" is precisely what the window audit tests; failing it means no d_g and no d_s.

### Inferences
- The three estimators disagree on exactly the graphs the project cares about. Volume-growth and spectral dimension both diverge for exponential growth (Bethe-like), while box-covering may still return a finite d_B on the same graph (Song et al. report finite d_B on networks whose ball growth is exponential). Reporting d_B alongside d_eff would therefore not "confirm" a dimension; it would measure something else (a renormalisation exponent). The honest cross-check for the project's question is the pair (d_eff from volume growth, d_s from return probability).
- No paper I found phrases the pitfall as "fixed-window slope on exponential growth gives a fake dimension". The content is nevertheless fully present: Csányi–Szendrői (d and α exclusive; 2–3 usable points at small N), Newman–Watts (D(r) = 4r/ξ grows without plateau), and Song et al. (d_f = ∞ for small-world growth). The project's window-stability audit is the operational implementation of Newman–Watts' D(r) curve; it should be cited as such.
- Robust choice for locally tree-like graphs: none of the slope estimators is "robust" — the correct answer is that the quantity does not exist. The robust *procedure* is to test for a plateau in D(r) (window audit) and, independently, to test for a Laplacian spectral gap / exponential decay of P(t) (Burioni–Cassi). A gap is a yes/no signal that does not depend on window choice.

### Gaps
- Could not read the Nat. Phys. 2011 full text (paywalled); the piecewise δ–d relation is not reproduced here.
- Shanker's exact definitions are from a secondary source (Wikipedia); the original MPLB text was not accessible.
- I found no paper that quantifies the *bias* of a two-point log-log slope as a function of window position for exponential growth beyond the Newman–Watts 1D formula; the 2D analogue of D(r) is only implicit in their self-consistent V(r) equation.

## Key Question 2 — Spectral dimension in CDT and in random trees/combs; what Hausdorff-vs-spectral tells us about tree-like graphs

### Takeaway
Spectral dimension is defined as D_S(σ) = −2 d log P(σ)/d log σ from the return probability of diffusion and is explicitly treated in quantum gravity as a *scale-dependent function*, not a number (CDT: D_S runs from ≈2 at short scales to 4.02 ± 0.1 at large scales; finite volume drives it to 0 at σ ≫ V^(2/d)). On random trees the two dimensions split: generic (critical, size-conditioned) random trees have d_H = 2 but d_s = 4/3, while supercritical/Bethe-like trees have both infinite (exponential growth, spectral gap). So "tree-like" is not one class: a critical tree has finite but *unequal* d_H and d_s; an exponentially growing tree has neither.

### Cited Findings
- **Ambjørn, Jurkiewicz & Loll (2005), "Spectral dimension of the universe", Phys. Rev. Lett. 95, 171301, arXiv hep-th/0505113.** Defines D_S(σ) = −2 d log P(σ)/d log σ with P(σ) the average return probability after diffusion time σ; best fit on σ ∈ [40, 400]: D_S(σ) = 4.02 − 119/(54 + σ) (general form a − b/(σ + c)), giving D_S(∞) = 4.02 ± 0.1 and D_S(0) = 1.80 ± 0.25; ensembles up to ≈181,000 four-simplices, σ_max = 400, 400 measurements; σ < 40 discarded for even–odd lattice artifacts; **finite volume: for σ ≫ V^(2/d) "the zero mode of the Laplacian will dominate" and D_S(σ) → 0**; error bars grow linearly in σ. — [Source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/hep-th/0505113). **Mapping: analogous.** This is the spectral-side twin of the window audit: short-scale lattice artifacts at one end, finite-size collapse at the other, and a fitted *curve* rather than a fixed window in between.
- **Benedetti & Henson (2009), "Spectral geometry as a probe of quantum spacetime", Phys. Rev. D 80, 124036, arXiv 0911.0401.** Uses the ensemble-averaged spectral dimension in 3D CDT as a diagnostic of the emergent geometry; finds the large-scale behaviour matches a Euclidean de Sitter sphere, with short-scale D_S ≈ 2 (dimensional reduction). — [Source](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/0911.0401). **Mapping: analogous** — demonstrates using D_S(σ) to *identify* the emergent geometry (compare against a candidate manifold's curve), not merely to read off a number.
- **Durhuus, Jonsson & Wheater (2007), "The spectral dimension of generic trees", J. Stat. Phys. 128, 1237–1260, arXiv math-ph/0607020, DOI 10.1007/s10955-007-9348-3.** Generic ensembles of infinite trees (limits of finite trees conditioned on size N, including uniformly bounded vertex order) are supported on trees with a single spine and have **d_s = 4/3 and d_H = 2**; mass exponent 1/3. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/math-ph/0607020). **Mapping: analogous.** Shows that a tree can have a perfectly good Hausdorff dimension (2) while diffusion sees 4/3 — the two instruments can disagree by a factor 1.5 on a tree with no shortcuts at all.
- **Durhuus, Jonsson & Wheater (2006), "On the spectral dimension of random trees", DMTCS Proc. AG (Fourth Colloquium on Mathematics and Computer Science), DOI 10.46298/dmtcs.3507.** Random combs (spine with linear-chain teeth) have spectral dimensions varying continuously in [1, 3/2]; generic random trees again 4/3. — [Source](https://dmtcs.episciences.org/3507). **Mapping: analogous** — a tunable tree family whose d_s can be dialled continuously; a candidate calibration target for any d_s estimator the project adds. The explicit comb formula d_s(α) was **[not verified; PDF not read]**.
- **Alexander & Orbach (1982), J. Physique Lett. 43, L625.** Introduced the spectral ("fracton") dimension through the vibrational density of states ρ(ω) ~ ω^(d_s − 1), with d_s = 2 d_f/(2 + θ) = 2 d_f/d_w (d_w = 2 + θ the walk dimension), and conjectured d_s ≈ 4/3 for percolation clusters in any d ≥ 2 (exact for d ≥ 6, where the incipient cluster is tree-like). — [Source](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevB.43.8183) (secondary); original letter not fetched. **Mapping: analogous.** The same 4/3 appears for critical percolation and for generic trees because both are critical tree-like objects; this is the natural reference value for the project's pruned-WS graphs near the percolation threshold.
- **Burioni & Cassi (2005)** (as above): Laplacian DOS at low eigenvalue ρ(l) ~ l^(d̄/2 − 1) and normal-mode density ρ(ω) ~ ω^(d̄ − 1); random walks recurrent for d̃ < 2, transient for d̃ > 2; Bethe lattice: infinite d_s, exponential return decay; on finite ergodic graphs the decay is no slower than p_2^t with p_2 the second-largest transition eigenvalue. — [Source](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0507142). **Mapping: direct** for the finite-graph statement: on a finite graph *everything* eventually decays exponentially (spectral gap of the finite chain), so d_s must be read in the window t ≪ 1/λ_2, just as d_H must be read at r ≪ diameter.

### Inferences
- "Hausdorff vs spectral on tree-like graphs" splits into two cases the project should name separately: (i) *critical* tree-like (uniform spanning trees, Galton–Watson conditioned on size, percolation clusters): d_H finite (2), d_s finite but smaller (4/3), ball growth polynomial — the window audit would pass; (ii) *supercritical / Bethe-like* (frontier growth with a degree cap > 2 and no size conditioning — the project's `grown`): exponential growth, infinite d_H and d_s, Laplacian gap. The 2026-09-26 audit result places `grown` in case (ii). A useful control is to generate case (i) graphs (e.g. uniform spanning tree of a 2D lattice, or a critical Galton–Watson tree conditioned on N) and confirm that the window audit passes with d_eff ≈ 2 while the return-probability slope gives ≈ 4/3.
- The CDT practice suggests the project should report d_eff(r) (and later D_S(t)) as a *curve with a fitted saturation form* (a − b/(c + r)) and treat the existence of the plateau a as the result; this is equivalent in spirit to the window audit but produces a comparable single-number summary and an uncertainty.

### Gaps
- Did not read the DJW comb paper's formula for d_s as a function of the tooth-length exponent.
- Could not confirm from a primary source an inequality d_s ≤ d_H for general graphs (true in the cited examples; Burioni–Cassi did not state one in the fetched text).

## Key Question 3 — Small-world dimension: Newman–Watts crossover length, shortcut density needed to destroy a d-dimensional ball-growth law, and whether "~20 shortcuts in 5000 nodes" matches

### Takeaway
Newman–Watts give ξ = (φ k d)^(−1/d) with τ = 1/d, a single length scale that separates d-dimensional growth (r ≪ ξ) from exponential growth (r ≫ ξ), and the crossover occurs when the *total* expected number of shortcuts φ k d L^d ≈ 1. Rewriting in terms of shortcut count S: ξ = (N/S)^(1/d), i.e. the typical lattice spacing between shortcut endpoints. For N = 5000, d = 2 this gives ξ ≈ 71 (S=1), 32 (S=5), 16 (S=20), 10 (S=50) — so S ≈ 20 is exactly where ξ falls inside a typical audit radius window (r ~ 10–20) on a 5000-node sheet. The literature predicts destruction, not inflation: beyond ξ the effective dimension grows linearly with r.

### Cited Findings
- **Newman & Watts (1999)** (as above): ξ = 1/(φ d)^(1/d) for k = 1 and ξ = 1/(φ k d)^(1/d) for general k; ξ ~ φ^(−τ) with τ = 1/d (τ = 1 in 1D); finite-size scaling ℓ = (L/k) f((φk)^(1/d) L) with f(x) ~ (log x)/x as x → ∞ and f(x) → d/4 as x → 0; crossover from large- to small-world "when the average number of shortcuts is about one, i.e. when φ k d L^d = 1", giving crossover length L = ξ; V(r) = (ξ/2)(e^(4r/ξ) − 1) in 1D; D(r) → 4r/ξ for r ≫ ξ. — [Source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/9904419). **Mapping: direct.**
- **Barthélémy & Amaral (1999), "Small-world networks: evidence for a crossover picture", Phys. Rev. Lett. 82, 3180.** Mean distance is a scaling function of n/n*, with crossover size n* ∝ p^(−τ), τ ≈ 2/3 at small p (later corrected toward τ = 1 in 1D by Newman–Watts and others). — [Source](https://amaral.northwestern.edu/publications/small-world-networks-evidence-for-a-crossover-picture). **Mapping: analogous** — historical note that finite-size fits of the crossover exponent were biased (2/3 vs the exact 1 in 1D), a warning for fitting crossover exponents at N ~ 10^3–10^4.
- **Csányi & Szendrői (2004)** (as above): Watts–Strogatz with small rewiring is fractal at small scales and small-world at large scales; a lattice of small-world "cities" is the reverse. — [Source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0406070). **Mapping: direct** for the two-scale picture behind finding (c).
- **Song, Havlin & Makse (2005)**: cluster-growing d_f = ∞ for small-world networks. — [Source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0503078). **Mapping: direct.**

### Inferences
- Converting Newman–Watts to the project's variables (derived here, not in the paper): S = φ k d L^d shortcuts, N = L^d, so **ξ = (N/S)^(1/d)**; in 2D, ξ = sqrt(N/S). Ball growth is d-dimensional only for r ≪ ξ. The window audit should start to fail when the upper edge of the radius window r_max reaches ~ξ, i.e. at **S* ≈ N / r_max^2** (2D). For N = 5000 and r_max ≈ 15–20, S* ≈ 12–22. The reported "~20 shortcuts in 5000 nodes" is therefore quantitatively consistent with Newman–Watts; this is a prediction the project can test by varying N and r_max (S* should scale as N/r_max^2, and for a 3D-like substrate as N/r_max^3).
- "Destroys rather than inflates": Newman–Watts' D(r) → 4r/ξ has no plateau, so no finite "inflated" dimension exists beyond ξ; a fixed window straddling ξ reports a drifting value. The Daqing et al. "dimension higher than embedding space" result is a different regime — a *power-law* link-length distribution gives a genuine new power law (finite d > 2), whereas uniform-random shortcuts (Newman–Watts/portals) give exponential growth (d = ∞). The project's portals are uniform-random, so Newman–Watts, not Daqing, is the applicable prediction.
- Finding (c) (smooth two-scale crossover in d(p)) is what Newman–Watts and Csányi–Szendrői both predict: there is no sharp transition at finite p; the apparent sharpness is controlled by where ξ(p) sits relative to the window.

### Gaps
- Newman–Watts give the closed-form V(r) and D(r) only for d = 1; the 2D D(r) must be obtained numerically from their self-consistent equation (or measured). I found no paper giving the 2D closed form.
- No paper directly studies "number of shortcuts at which a finite-window volume-growth slope becomes unstable"; the S* ≈ N/r_max^d estimate above is my inference from ξ.

## Key Question 4 — Random and quantum walks on trees vs lattices: hitting/cover times, localization on trees, glued-trees speedup; is "100x gain from shortcuts on tree-like, none on 2D" known?

### Takeaway
On trees, classical hitting/commute times are exponential in depth (commute time = 2m·R_eff with R_eff = path length and m ~ b^h edges), and on glued trees the classical walker needs exponentially many steps while the symmetric quantum walk crosses in poly(n) — but any randomness (on-site disorder, or breaking the column symmetry) kills the quantum advantage via Anderson localization in the effective 1D column chain. CTQWs on Cayley trees/dendrimers localize near the starting branch, and shortcuts added to a ring make quantum transport fast but *prevent* equipartition. So "shortcuts relieve localization on a tree-like substrate" is consistent with known mechanisms (bottleneck removal classically; breaking branch confinement quantum-mechanically), while the near-zero 2D gain is consistent with 2D lattices already having polynomial (ballistic for CTQW, diffusive for CTRW) transport — but I found no paper that reports the specific 100x-vs-1x contrast.

### Cited Findings
- **Childs, Cleve, Deotto, Farhi, Gutmann & Spielman (2003), "Exponential algorithmic speedup by quantum walk", STOC 2003 pp. 59–68, DOI 10.1145/780542.780552, arXiv quant-ph/0209131.** Glued trees G_n: two depth-n binary trees joined by a random cycle alternating between the leaves; the column subspace (2n+2 states) is invariant under H, reducing the CTQW to a line with hopping 1 and a √2 defect at the middle (γ = 1/√2); Theorem 3: for large n, a measurement at a time uniform in [0, n^4/(2ε)] finds EXIT with probability > (1−ε)/(2n), so poly(n) queries suffice; Theorem 9: any classical algorithm with ≤ 2^(n/6) queries succeeds with probability ≤ 4·2^(−n/6); for the simpler identified-leaves graph "a classical random walk starting at the left root requires exponentially many steps (in n) to reach the right root". — [Source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/quant-ph/0209131). **Mapping: analogous.** Shows that on a tree-like substrate the walk-type contrast is maximal, and that the quantum advantage depends on an exact symmetry (column space), which random shortcuts would break.
- **Keating, Linden, Matthews & Winter (2007), "Localization and its consequences for quantum walk algorithms and quantum communication", Phys. Rev. A 76, 012315, arXiv quant-ph/0606205.** On the Childs–Farhi–Gutmann graph with Cauchy-distributed on-site disorder of width δ, the column-space walk is an Anderson chain: maximum localization length l_max ~ √8γ/δ, so once n ≳ √8γ/δ the probability of reaching the far root is exponentially small in n; for finite-variance disorder l ~ δ^(−2); "quantum particles can be exponentially delayed even from reaching the centre, unlike classical diffusion, which reaches the centre quickly"; conclusion: "quantum walks on physical graphs are not likely to be useful for algorithmic purposes". — [Source](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/quant-ph/0606205). **Mapping: analogous.** Quantum localization on tree-like graphs is generic under imperfection; the project's "no-shortcut walker 30–100x below equipartition" on `grown` is the same phenomenology if the degree-cap heterogeneity acts as effective disorder [speculative].
- **Jackson, Khoo & Strauch (2012), "Quantum walks on trees with disorder: decay, diffusion, and localization", Phys. Rev. A 86, 022335, arXiv 1206.3178.** Glued trees (N_d = 3·2^d − 2) with Anderson on-site disorder W: weak W gives quantum *decay* out of the column space, p_hit ≈ p_d exp[−(1/16)(d − 1/2)W^2] with p_d ~ d^(−2/3) at zero disorder; intermediate W ≈ 2 gives a quantum-to-classical crossover to diffusive transport, T_c = T_0/(1 + c(W/γ)^2) (T_0 = 0.8, c = 0.2); strong W_c ≈ 15–17 gives full localization (IPR → 0.5), matching the Cayley tree/Bethe lattice; with disorder the hitting probability decays exponentially in depth with mean free path ℓ ~ 1/W^2; "intermediate disorder will inhibit any quantum speedup". — [Source](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1206.3178). **Mapping: analogous.** Gives three regimes and the scalings to compare the project's quantum-walk hitting observable against.
- **Mülken, Pernice & Blumen (2007), "Quantum transport on small-world networks: a continuous-time quantum walk approach", Phys. Rev. E 76, 051125, arXiv 0705.1608.** Ring of N nodes plus B random bonds, H = Laplacian, π_kj(t) = |⟨k|e^(−iHt)|j⟩|^2, N = 100, B = 1, 2, 5, 100, 500 realisations; ring baseline long-time average χ̄_ring = (2N−2)/N^2 = 0.0198; as B grows the long-time averaged return probability *rises above* the equipartition value 1/N and transport becomes fast (limiting value reached quickly vs ~N/2 for the ring); classical walks reach 1/N quickly, quantum walks do not: "on average the exciton is most likely to be found at the initial node"; not Anderson localization (all transition probabilities stay nonzero); χ̄_R = (1/RN)Σ|⟨j|Φ⟩|^4 is a participation ratio; for N = 500, 1000 χ̄_R peaks near B/N ≈ 0.14. — [Source](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/0705.1608). **Mapping: direct for the design** (random extra bonds on a low-dimensional substrate, classical vs quantum, equipartition as the yardstick). Their result that shortcuts speed up quantum transport but leave it *further* from equipartition is a specific prediction the project can check on its 2D sheet.
- **Mülken & Blumen (2011), "Continuous-time quantum walks: models for coherent transport on complex networks", Phys. Rep. 502, 37–87, arXiv 1101.2572.** Average return probability π̄(t) ≥ |ᾱ(t)|^2 = |(1/N)Σ_n e^(−iE_n t)|^2; for DOS ρ(E) ~ E^ν at small E, CTRW p̄(t) ~ t^(−(1+ν)) and CTQW envelope ~ t^(−2(1+ν)), i.e. with d_s = 2(1+ν): classical t^(−d_s/2), quantum t^(−d_s) (ring: t^(−1/2) vs t^(−1)); 2D lattice CTQW is exact in Bessel functions π(t) = [J_{kx−jx}(2t) J_{ky−jy}(2t)]^2 (ballistic); dendrimers (f = 3, N = 3·2^G − 2): starting at the outer generation "the main fraction of π_kj(t) stays in a small region closely connected by bonds to the initial node", transfer to other branches very unlikely even at long times; glued Cayley trees: top-to-bottom fast, left-to-right very slow; classical long-time limit is 1/N regardless of connectivity, quantum long-time average "remembers" the initial condition. — [Source](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1101.2572). **Mapping: direct** for the CTQW-on-tree localization and for the quantum/classical exponent relation (d_s vs d_s/2), which is a second route to d_s.
- **Mülken, Bierbaum & Blumen (2006), "Coherent exciton transport in dendrimers and continuous-time quantum walks", J. Chem. Phys. 124, 124905, arXiv cond-mat/0602040.** CTQW on dendrimers: perfect recurrences up to generation 2 from the centre; for larger dendrimers transport to certain nodes has very low probability depending on the start site; Cauchy–Schwarz lower bound on the averaged return probability depending only on the spectrum. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0602040). **Mapping: analogous** — branch confinement on Cayley-tree-like graphs.
- **Chandra, Raghavan, Ruzzo, Smolensky & Tiwari (1989/1996), "The electrical resistance of a graph captures its commute and cover times", STOC 1989; Comput. Complexity 6(4), 312–340.** Commute time between s and t equals 2m·R_st (m edges, unit resistors); cover time satisfies mR ≤ C ≤ O(mR log n) with R the maximal effective resistance. — [Source](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF01270385). **Mapping: direct tool.** On a tree the unique path gives R_st = dist(s,t), so for a b-ary tree of depth h the root–leaf commute time is 2m·h with m ≈ b^(h+1)/(b−1): exponential in depth (derived here). Adding one shortcut edge lowers R_eff between the regions it connects by at most a factor set by the parallel path; on a tree this can be a huge relative change because there was exactly one path, on a 2D lattice R_eff between distant points already grows only like log(distance) so a shortcut changes little — a concrete, testable mechanism for the 100x vs 1.0–1.4x contrast.
- **Cortines, Louidor & Saglietti (2021), "A scaling limit for the cover time of the binary tree", Adv. Math.** Cover time of the depth-n binary tree is of order 2^(n+1)·n with Gumbel fluctuations after centring by (log 2)n − log n. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.10101). **Mapping: analogous** — cover time ~ N log N on a tree, i.e. the *cover* time is not the pathological quantity; the pathological ones are hitting/commute times across bottlenecks.

### Inferences
- The project's finding (b) decomposes into two known mechanisms: (classical) trees have a single path between regions, so one shortcut collapses R_eff and hence commute time by orders of magnitude, whereas on a 2D lattice R_eff ~ (1/π) log r and shortcuts barely matter until ξ < diameter; (quantum) on tree-like graphs the walk is confined to the starting branch (Mülken–Blumen dendrimer results) and a shortcut between branches releases it, while on 2D the CTQW is already ballistic. The 30–100x deficit below equipartition without shortcuts on `grown` is what the dendrimer results predict qualitatively. I did not find a paper that reports this exact contrast as a single measurement.
- The glued-trees exponential speedup is **not** the right analogue for the project's shortcut gain: that speedup comes from symmetry and is destroyed by randomness (Keating et al.; Jackson et al.). The project's gain comes from *adding* randomness (shortcuts) to a localized walker — the opposite direction — and is better compared with Mülken–Pernice–Blumen's small-world CTQW.
- Mülken–Pernice–Blumen predict a specific non-monotonicity: quantum long-time return probability peaks at B/N ≈ 0.14 and stays above 1/N. If the project's "equipartition" observable is the long-time averaged occupation, the quantum walker on the shortcut-decorated 2D sheet should remain *below* equipartition even when fast — a check that the project's 1.0–1.4x is measuring speed rather than spread.

### Gaps
- No source found that treats quantum walks on *degree-capped frontier-grown* graphs or on random regular graphs as walks (only the Anderson tight-binding model on random regular graphs: Liu & Lopatto 2026 [arXiv 2606.24671], Tikhonov & Mirlin 2019 [PRB 100, 094201], not fetched in full).
- The hitting-time derivation for a b-ary tree above is my own application of the commute-time identity; Levin–Peres–Wilmer's textbook treatment was not fetched.

## Key Question 5 — Connectivity thresholds: is transport turning on at ~2–3 edge-disjoint paths a known result? Universal small count at which a ball boundary becomes connected to the bulk?

### Takeaway
No. The literature has three nearby results — Menger's theorem (max number of edge-disjoint s–t paths equals the minimum edge cut, an integer), Bollobás–Thomason (in the random graph process, k-connectivity arrives at exactly the moment minimum degree reaches k), and Coniglio's red-bond theorem (singly connected bonds on the percolation backbone have fractal dimension 1/ν; one red bond carries the whole current) — but I found no paper identifying a universal ≈2.6-strand threshold for a transport observable through a ball boundary. The project's number is most naturally read as an ensemble average of integer thresholds (2 or 3) set by Menger-type cuts.

### Cited Findings
- **Bollobás & Thomason (1985), "Random graphs of small order", Ann. Discrete Math. 28, 47–97 [exact reference unverified; cited via secondary sources].** In the random graph process on K_n, for every 1 ≤ k ≤ n−1 the hitting times for minimum degree k, k-edge-connectivity and k-vertex-connectivity coincide with high probability; the deterministic direction (k-connected ⇒ min degree ≥ k) is trivial, the content is the converse. Extended to d-regular base graphs (2024) and hypergraphs (Poole). — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14398); [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1008.1865). **Mapping: analogous.** Connectivity thresholds are governed by the *weakest local spot*, not a global count — consistent with the project's companion finding that the sheet's crossover is triggered by a single dead corner.
- **Coniglio (1981/1982), PRL 46, 250 and J. Phys. A 15, 3829 [exact titles unverified].** Rigorous result that the fractal dimension of singly connected ("red") bonds on the percolation backbone is d_red = 1/ν (< 1 in 2D); confirmed to O(ε^4) in field theory; red ⊂ backbone ⊂ cluster with d_red ≤ d_BB ≤ d_f. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0007129); [Source](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-59900-2_8). **Pike & Stanley (1981), J. Phys. A 14, L169**: partition of backbone bonds into red (singly connected), blue (multiply connected), yellow (dangling). — [Source](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/fafwk-d6h04). **Mapping: analogous.** The number of red bonds crossing a box of size L scales as L^(1/ν) (≈ L^(3/4) in 2D), which is "a small count that grows slowly" — the closest thing to a universal small strand count, but it is a scaling law, not a fixed number like 2.6.
- **Menger's theorem (1927)** — standard graph theory: the maximum number of edge-disjoint paths between two vertex sets equals the minimum number of edges whose removal disconnects them. [Textbook result; not fetched.] **Mapping: direct** — the project's "edge-disjoint strands threading a ball's boundary" is exactly the Menger quantity for (ball interior, exterior), so per-sample thresholds must be integers.
- **Mülken & Blumen (2011)**: on glued Cayley trees, transport top-to-bottom is fast while left-to-right is very slow — i.e. transport through a region depends on how many independent routes thread it. — [Source](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1101.2572). **Mapping: speculative.**

### Inferences
- A "universal ≈2.6" is suspicious as a single number because Menger counts are integers. The natural hypothesis: for each ball the observable turns on at exactly 2 or exactly 3 strands, and the mix (≈60% at 3, 40% at 2) is substrate-independent. This is testable from the project's existing data by histogramming per-ball integer thresholds instead of averaging.
- Why 2–3 rather than 1: with one strand the boundary is a red bond — the interior is a dangling cluster and transport is gated by a single edge (Coniglio); with ≥2 strands the interior joins a cycle through the bulk and the effective resistance between interior and bulk drops from "series" to "parallel". That ≥2 is the minimal non-dangling case is a Menger/red-bond statement, not a dynamical universality; whether the project's observable needs 2 or 3 would then depend on the observable's capacity scale, which the project reports it does not — this is the genuinely unexplained part.

### Gaps
- No literature found on a dynamical/transport threshold at a small fixed number of edge-disjoint paths across a ball boundary. This appears to be an open or unexplored point; the project's result may be novel or may be an averaging artifact (see inference above).
- Coniglio's original papers were not fetched; d_red = 1/ν is confirmed only via citing sources.

## Key Question 6 — Laplacian spectral features of locally tree-like / degree-capped graphs: Kesten–McKay, Lorentzian/Cauchy features, lattice vs tree low-eigenvalue signatures

### Takeaway
For random d-regular graphs the adjacency spectrum converges to the Kesten–McKay law ρ(x) = d·√(4(d−1) − x²)/(2π(d² − x²)) on |x| ≤ 2√(d−1), so the Laplacian L = d − A has a **gap** [d − 2√(d−1), d + 2√(d−1)] above zero — the spectral signature of infinite spectral dimension. A d-dimensional lattice instead has ρ(λ) ~ λ^(d/2 − 1) down to λ = 0 (constant DOS in 2D). Degree heterogeneity (as in a degree-capped grown graph) adds localized states at the band edges (Lifshitz tails), δ-peaks from local motifs, and isolated eigenvalues — the ingredients for "spikes". The Kesten–McKay density itself carries a Lorentzian-like factor 1/(d² − x²), but I found no source describing "Lorentzian spikes" in Laplacian spectra of tree-like graphs; the Cauchy connection in the literature is to the *distribution of the local Green's function* on the Bethe lattice (Abou-Chacra–Anderson–Thouless), not to the DOS.

### Cited Findings
- **McKay (1981), "The expected eigenvalue distribution of a large regular graph", Linear Algebra Appl. 40, 203–216 [volume/pages unverified from primary source].** For sequences of d-regular graphs that are locally tree-like (few short cycles), the empirical adjacency spectrum converges to Kesten's measure: ρ_d(x) = d·√(4(d−1) − x²)_+ / (2π(d² − x²)); in normalized form (H = A/√(d−1)) ρ(x) = (1 + 1/(d−1) − x²/d)^(−1) √(4 − x²)_+ /(2π); the density is the Plancherel measure of the infinite d-regular tree. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.05297); [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1609.09052); [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.00113). **Mapping: direct** for any degree-capped graph whose typical node sits in a tree-like neighbourhood (the cap makes the degree nearly regular). Bauerschmidt–Huang–Yau (2016) prove a *local* Kesten–McKay law and bulk eigenvector delocalization for random regular graphs. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.09052v1).
- **Burioni & Cassi (2005)**: Laplacian DOS ρ(l) ~ l^(d̄/2 − 1) at small eigenvalue; Bethe lattice has infinite spectral dimension and exponentially decaying return probability. — [Source](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0507142). **Mapping: direct.** Combined with Kesten–McKay: ρ(λ) ~ √(λ − λ_min) at the lower band edge λ_min = d − 2√(d−1) > 0 (= 3 − 2√2 ≈ 0.17 for d = 3). A fit of ρ(λ) ~ λ^(d_s/2−1) that ignores the offset λ_min can return a spurious finite d_s (a square-root edge mimics d_s = 3) — the spectral analogue of the fixed-window pitfall [inference].
- **Kühn (2008), "Spectra of sparse random matrices", J. Phys. A 41, 295002, arXiv 0803.2886.** Cavity/replica solution for spectra of sparse random matrices including graph Laplacians (row constraints); identifies pure-point (δ-peak) contributions signalling normalizable localized eigenstates; unfolds the DOS by local coordination; well-connected sites dominate the localized states in the lower band-edge Lifshitz tails; the δ-peak at λ = 0 of the Laplacian comes from isolated vertices/components. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0803.2886); [Source](https://nms.kcl.ac.uk/reimer.kuehn/published/jpa08.pdf). **Mapping: direct tool** for the project's `grown` Laplacian spectra: the cavity method predicts, per local coordination, where localized states and δ-peaks sit.
- **Aspelmeier & Zippelius (2011), "The integrated density of states of the random graph Laplacian", J. Stat. Phys. 144, 759, arXiv 1008.1087.** Laplacian of the Erdős–Rényi graph has Lifshitz tails in the integrated DOS for any finite mean degree c; the degeneracy of λ = 0 equals the number of components; near the spectral edge the dominant contribution comes from sparse, weakly branched subgraphs; strong evidence for discrete eigenvalues across the whole spectrum; semicircle recovered as c → ∞. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1008.1087). **Mapping: analogous.** "Discrete eigenvalues across the whole spectrum" from rare local motifs is a candidate explanation for spikes.
- **Farkas, Derényi, Barabási & Vicsek (2001), "Spectra of 'real-world' graphs: beyond the semicircle law", Phys. Rev. E 64, 026704, arXiv cond-mat/0102335.** Adjacency (not Laplacian) spectra: sparse uncorrelated random graphs do not converge to the semicircle; scale-free graphs have a triangle-like density with power-law tails; **small-world (WS) graphs have "a complex spectral density function consisting of several sharp peaks"** inherited from the regular ring. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0102335). **Mapping: analogous** — sharp peaks in sparse, locally regular graphs are the norm, not an anomaly.
- **Abou-Chacra, Anderson & Thouless (1973), "A selfconsistent theory of localization", J. Phys. C 6, 1734.** Self-consistent (Bethe–Peierls-exact) recursion for the local Green's function on the Bethe lattice; extended vs localized states distinguished by whether the imaginary part of the self-energy/hybridisation stays finite in a band; mobility edge depends on disorder. — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1002.3696) (secondary); original not fetched. **Mapping: speculative.** This is where Cauchy/Lorentzian distributions enter tree-lattice spectral theory (as the stable law for the local Green's function under the recursion), not as DOS line shapes. **[Cauchy-stable-law detail unverified from the 1973 paper.]**
- **Alon–Boppana / Friedman** (not fetched): finite d-regular graphs have second adjacency eigenvalue ≥ 2√(d−1) − o(1), and random ones achieve ≈ 2√(d−1) + ε, so the finite-N Laplacian gap is ≈ d − 2√(d−1) — the gap is not a finite-size artifact. **[Standard result; marked unverified here.]**

### Inferences
- The low-λ test that separates "2D sheet" from "tree-like" is binary and window-free: a 2D sheet's Laplacian DOS is flat (ρ ~ λ^0) down to λ_2 ~ 1/N (Weyl: λ_k ~ (k/N)^(2/d)), while a degree-capped tree-like graph has (almost) no eigenvalues between λ_2 and the Kesten–McKay edge ≈ (√(d−1) − 1)² — the only eigenvalues below the edge are localized Lifshitz-tail states on rare motifs. Counting eigenvalues in (0, λ_edge) per node is therefore a cleaner "has a dimension" diagnostic than any slope.
- Candidate explanations for the project's "Lorentzian spikes", in order of plausibility and each checkable with an inverse participation ratio: (i) δ-peaks from local motifs (e.g. two leaves on one parent give Laplacian eigenvalue exactly 1; symmetric motifs give integer eigenvalues) broadened by weak coupling — Kühn's pure-point contributions; (ii) Lifshitz-tail localized states near the band edges (Aspelmeier–Zippelius); (iii) resonance line shapes from a localized state hybridizing with a continuum — this would genuinely be Lorentzian (Breit–Wigner), and it is what a motif eigenvalue inside the Kesten–McKay band would produce [speculative]. "Spectral flow" with growth is then the motion of these motif/localized eigenvalues as the local structure changes.

### Gaps
- No paper found that reports Lorentzian line shapes in the Laplacian DOS of tree-like or degree-capped graphs; the Lorentzian/Cauchy connection in the literature is to Green's-function distributions.
- McKay 1981 bibliographic details and the Alon–Boppana bound are from memory/secondary sources, not fetched primary text.

## Key Question 7 — New paths: estimator changes, controls, and tests the project could run

### Takeaway
Six concrete, paper-grounded additions: (1) a spectral-dimension instrument from return probability / Laplacian DOS with the CDT-style running-dimension fit; (2) a gap-count diagnostic from Kesten–McKay; (3) a Newman–Watts scaling test of the ~20-shortcut threshold (S* ∝ N/r_max^d); (4) critical-tree controls where d_H = 2 and d_s = 4/3 must come apart; (5) effective-resistance bookkeeping to explain the 100x/1x walk contrast; (6) an integer-threshold histogram and IPR test for the 2.6-strand and Lorentzian-spike results.

### Cited Findings
- **Path 1 — Add spectral dimension as a second instrument, reported as a running curve.** Compute P(t) = (1/N) Σ_n e^(−λ_n t) from the Laplacian spectrum (N ≤ 10^4 is directly diagonalizable; for larger N use stochastic trace estimation or many random-walk samples) and D_S(t) = −2 d log P/d log t; fit a − b/(c + t) over t_min ≪ t ≪ 1/λ_2 as in Ambjørn–Jurkiewicz–Loll — [Source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/hep-th/0505113); equivalently fit ρ(λ) ~ λ^(d_s/2−1) or Weyl λ_k ~ (k/N)^(2/d_s) (Fiedler scaling λ_2 ~ N^(−2/d_s)) — [Source](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0507142); [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.19104). Prediction: 2D sheet → plateau at 2 in both d_eff and D_S; `grown` → d_eff drifts (Newman–Watts-style) and D_S(t) shows exponential P(t) with no plateau. The CTQW version gives the exponent twice: CTRW t^(−d_s/2) vs CTQW envelope t^(−d_s) — [Source](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1101.2572) — a self-consistency check that uses the project's existing quantum-walk code.
- **Path 2 — Spectral-gap count as a window-free "has a dimension" flag.** For each run, count Laplacian eigenvalues in (0, λ_edge) with λ_edge = (√(k̄−1) − 1)² from the Kesten–McKay support at the mean degree k̄ — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.05297). A 2D sheet has ~N·λ_edge·const eigenvalues there (flat DOS); a Bethe-like graph has only O(number of rare motifs). Tag each sub-edge eigenvector with its inverse participation ratio to separate extended (dimension-bearing) from localized (Lifshitz-tail) states — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0803.2886); [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1008.1087). Caveat from Burioni–Cassi: fit DOS against (λ − λ_min), not λ, or a square-root edge fakes d_s = 3.
- **Path 3 — Test the shortcut threshold against ξ = (N/S)^(1/d).** Newman–Watts give crossover when the expected shortcut count is O(1) and ξ = (φkd)^(−1/d), i.e. ξ = (N/S)^(1/d) — [Source](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/9904419). Prediction: the window audit fails when the window's upper radius reaches ξ, so the destabilising shortcut count S* scales as N/r_max^2 on 2D substrates (≈ 12–22 for N = 5000, r_max = 15–20, matching the observed ~20). Run N ∈ {2000, 5000, 20000} × r_max ∈ {10, 15, 25} and check the collapse; also check that d_eff(r) beyond ξ grows ∝ r (not to a new constant), which is the "destroys, not inflates" signature in closed form.
- **Path 4 — Critical-tree controls that separate the two tree classes.** Generate (a) a uniform spanning tree of the 2D sheet, (b) a critical Galton–Watson tree conditioned on size N, and (c) `grown` with cap 2 (a path). Durhuus–Jonsson–Wheater predict d_H = 2, d_s = 4/3 for (b) — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/math-ph/0607020) — and Alexander–Orbach predict d_s ≈ 4/3 for pruned-WS graphs at the percolation threshold — [Source](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevB.43.8183). If the project's d(p) curve passes through the percolation point, the spectral instrument should show 4/3 there, which would turn the "smooth crossover" into a curve with a known interior fixed point. The window audit should *pass* on (a)–(b) with d_eff ≈ 2, demonstrating that the audit distinguishes critical-tree-like from Bethe-like rather than rejecting all trees.
- **Path 5 — Explain the 100x vs 1.0–1.4x walk contrast with effective resistance.** Commute time = 2m·R_eff — [Source](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/BF01270385). Compute R_eff between the walk's source/target before and after each portal (pseudo-inverse of L; cheap at N ≤ 10^4). Prediction: on `grown`, R_eff = path length (single path) and one portal cuts it by a factor comparable to the observed gain; on the 2D sheet, R_eff ~ (1/π) log r and the same portal changes it by O(10%). This converts (b) from an observation into a derived number and tests Mülken–Pernice–Blumen's prediction that the quantum walker on a shortcut-decorated ring/sheet stays *above* 1/N return probability (below equipartition) even when fast — [Source](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/0705.1608).
- **Path 6 — Integer thresholds and IPR for the two anomalies.** For the 2.6-strand result, histogram the per-ball integer Menger count at turn-on (edge-disjoint paths = min edge cut): if it is a 2/3 mixture, the "universal 2.6" is a mixing fraction, and the question becomes what determines the fraction (cf. Bollobás–Thomason: thresholds set by the weakest local spot — [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14398); Coniglio: a single red bond gates transport — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0007129)). For the Lorentzian spikes, compute IPR and the supporting motif for each spike eigenvector and test whether spike centres coincide with motif eigenvalues (e.g. exactly 1 for leaf pairs) following Kühn's pure-point decomposition — [Source](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0803.2886); a Lorentzian width ∝ coupling of the motif to the bulk would confirm a resonance interpretation.

### Inferences
- Paths 1–2 cost one Laplacian diagonalisation per run and reuse the existing pickle; they are the cheapest way to make the "no dimension" verdict independent of the window choice.
- Path 3 is the only one that can be falsified against an existing number (~20 shortcuts); if S* does not scale as N/r_max^2 the portal mechanism is not Newman–Watts-like and that would itself be a result.

### Gaps
- Path 4's 4/3 prediction assumes the pruned-WS crossover passes near a percolation point; whether the project's d(p) sweep does so is not established in the sources I read.
- No source gives a finite-N correction formula for D_S(t) on graphs of N ~ 5000; the usable window t_min ≪ t ≪ 1/λ_2 may be narrow (AJL discarded σ < 40 and saw error bars grow linearly in σ).
